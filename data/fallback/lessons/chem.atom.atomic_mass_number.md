## The idea  
The **atomic number** (Z) counts the protons in an atom’s nucleus.  
The **mass number** (A) counts both protons and neutrons:  
$$A = Z + N$$  
where N is the number of neutrons.  
The atomic mass you see on the periodic table (≈ 12.01 u for carbon) is an average of all naturally occurring isotopes; it is not the same as the integer mass number.

## Why your answer happened  
You likely mixed up the atomic mass with the mass number, thinking the decimal value on the periodic table was the exact count of protons + neutrons. That feels reasonable because the table shows a single number, but that number represents a weighted average over all isotopes, not a specific count for one atom.

## Analogy  
Think of a sports team roster.  
- The **team’s name** (e.g., “Lakers”) is like the **element’s symbol**.  
- The **number of starters** (players who always play) is like the **atomic number** (protons).  
- The **total team roster** (starters + bench players) is like the **mass number** (protons + neutrons).  
Even if the team’s average salary (atomic mass) is a decimal, the roster count is an integer.

## Worked example  
Consider carbon‑12.  
- Protons, Z = 6.  
- Neutrons, N = 6 (since 12 – 6 = 6).  
- Mass number, A = Z + N = 6 + 6 = 12.  
The atomic mass listed (≈ 12.01 u) is close to 12 because the most common isotope is carbon‑12, but the exact integer is 12.

## Try this  
What is the mass number of an atom that has 8 protons and 10 neutrons?  
<details><summary>Answer</summary>  
$A = 8 + 10 = 18$
