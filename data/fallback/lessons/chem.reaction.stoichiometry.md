## The idea
In a balanced chemical equation the numbers in front of each compound are the **mole ratios**.  
If the equation is

\[
aA + bB \;\longrightarrow\; cC + dD
\]

then \(a\) moles of \(A\) react with \(b\) moles of \(B\) to give \(c\) moles of \(C\) and \(d\) moles of \(D\).  
To convert a known amount of one substance to another, divide by its coefficient and multiply by the coefficient of the desired substance:

\[
n_{\text{desired}} = n_{\text{known}}\;\frac{\text{coeff}_{\text{desired}}}{\text{coeff}_{\text{known}}}
\]

## Why your answer happened
Many students treat the coefficients as “weights” instead of ratios.  
For example, when asked how many grams of product come from 5 g of reactant, a common mistake is to multiply 5 g by the product’s coefficient and divide by the reactant’s coefficient.  
This feels reasonable because it mirrors the “multiply‑by‑something, divide‑by‑something” pattern, but it ignores the fact that the coefficients express *moles*, not mass. The error stems from confusing **mole‑to‑mass conversion** with the **ratio conversion** that stoichiometry requires.

## Analogy
Think of a soccer team lineup.  
The coefficient is like the number of players in each position.  
If you want to know how many defenders (product) you have when you have 10 midfielders (reactant), you use the ratio of defenders to midfielders from the team sheet, not the total number of players.  
So, “defenders per midfielder” is the mole ratio, not the total roster size.

## Worked example
Balanced equation:

\[
\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}
\]

Given: \(4.0\;\text{g}\;\ce{H2}\).  
Molar masses: \(M_{\ce{H2}}=2.02\;\text{g/mol}\), \(M_{\ce{H2O}}=18.02\;\text{g/mol}\).

1. Convert grams to moles of \(\ce{H2}\):

\[
n_{\ce{H2}} = \frac{4.0\;\text{g}}{2.02\;\text{g/mol}} = 1.98\;\text{mol}
\]

2. Use the mole ratio \(\frac{n_{\ce{H2O}}}{n_{\ce{H2}}} = \frac{2}{2}=1\):

\[
n_{\ce{H2O}} = 1.98\;\text{mol}
\]

3. Convert moles of \(\ce{H2O}\) to grams:

\[
m_{\ce{H2O}} = 1.98\;\text{mol}\times 18.02\;\text{g/mol}=35.7\;\text{g}
\]

So, \(35.7\;\text{g}\) of water are produced.

## Try this
How many grams of \(\ce{CO2}\) are produced from \(10.0\;\text{g}\) of \(\ce{CH4}\) in the reaction

\[
\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}
\]?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
\[
\text{Molar masses: } M_{\ce{CH4}}=16.04,\; M_{\ce{CO2}}=44.01
\]
\[
n_{\ce{CH4}}=\frac{10.0}{16.04}=0.624\;\text{mol}
\]
Ratio \(\frac{n_{\ce{CO2}}}{n_{\ce{CH4}}}=1\)
\[
n_{\ce{CO2}}=0.624\;\text{mol}
\]
\[
m_{\ce{CO2}}=0.624\times44.01=27.5\;\text{g}
\]
So, \(27.5\;\text{g}\) of \(\ce{CO2}\) are produced.
