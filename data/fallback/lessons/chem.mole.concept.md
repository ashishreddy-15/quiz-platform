## The idea
A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, but for atoms, molecules, or any tiny particles. One mole contains exactly $6.02\times10^{23}$ entities; this number is called Avogadro’s number. When we say “2 moles of carbon atoms,” we mean $2\times6.02\times10^{23}$ carbon atoms. The mole lets us connect the mass we can weigh in the lab to the number of particles we are actually dealing with.

## Why your answer happened
You wrote that “one mole is the mass of a substance in grams,” which is a common mix‑up. It feels reasonable because the gram‑mole (the molar mass) is often introduced right after the mole definition, so the two ideas can blur together. The mole itself is a count, while the molar mass tells us how many grams correspond to that count for a particular substance.

## Analogy
Think of a basketball team roster.  
- **One mole** is like the **total number of players allowed on a roster** – a fixed count (e.g., 15 players).  
- **Avogadro’s number ($6.02\times10^{23}$)** is the exact number of roster spots.  
- **Molar mass** is like the **average height of the players**; it tells you how heavy the whole roster is in grams, but the roster size stays the same.

## Worked example
**Problem:** How many molecules are in $3.0\ \text{g}$ of water (H₂O)? (Molar mass of water = $18.0\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}$)

1. Convert grams to moles:  
   $$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{3.0\ \text{g}}{18.0\ \text{g·mol}^{-1}} = 0.167\ \text{mol}$$
2. Convert moles to number of molecules using Avogadro’s number:  
   $$N = n \times N_A = 0.167\ \text{mol}\times 6.02\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$$  
   $$N \approx 1.0\times10^{23}\ \text{molecules}$$

So $3.0\ \text{g}$ of water contains about $1.0\times10^{23}$ water molecules.
