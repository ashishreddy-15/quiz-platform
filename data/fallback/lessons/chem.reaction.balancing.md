## The idea  
In a chemical equation the total number of atoms of each element must be the same on the left and on the right.  
The only thing you can change is the *coefficients* (the numbers in front of formulas).  
Subscripts inside the formulas are fixed; they tell you how many atoms are in one molecule.

## Why your answer happened  
Many students think they can change the subscripts, for example turning \( \text{H}_2\text{O} \) into \( \text{H}_3\text{O} \).  
That feels reasonable because the equation still looks balanced, but it actually changes the composition of the substance.  
The correct approach is to keep the subscripts and adjust only the coefficients.

## Analogy  
Think of a basketball team lineup.  
The *players* are like the elements; their *positions* (guards, forwards, centers) are the subscripts inside the formula.  
You cannot change a player’s position during the game.  
The *coach* can only decide how many players of each type (coefficients) will be on the court to make sure the team has the right balance of positions on both halves of the court.

## Worked example  
Balance \( \text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \).

1. Count atoms:  
   Left: Fe = 1, O = 2  
   Right: Fe = 2, O = 3  

2. Adjust Fe with coefficient 2 on the left:  
   \(2\,\text{Fe} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\).  
   Now Fe: 2 on both sides.

3. O atoms: left 2, right 3.  
   Multiply \(\text{O}_2\) by 3:  
   \(2\,\text{Fe} + 3\,\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3\).

4. Check:  
   Fe: 2 = 2, O: 6 = 6.  
   The equation is balanced.
