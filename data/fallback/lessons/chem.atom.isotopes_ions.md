## The idea
An **isotope** is a version of the same element that has the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. The number of protons determines the element’s identity, while the extra neutrons change the atom’s mass.  

An **ion** is an atom (or molecule) that has lost or gained electrons, so its total charge is not zero. The number of protons stays the same, but the electron count is different from the proton count, giving a positive or negative charge.

## Why your answer happened
You wrote that isotopes and ions are the same because both “have a different number of particles.” That feels reasonable—both involve a change from the neutral atom. The mix‑up occurs because the particles that change are different: neutrons for isotopes, electrons for ions. Keeping the particle type straight clears the confusion.

## Analogy
Think of a basketball team:

- **Protons** are the players on the roster – they define which team it is.  
- **Neutrons** are the players’ jersey numbers – they don’t change the team, but they make each player unique and affect the team’s overall weight. Changing a jersey number (adding or removing neutrons) gives a different **isotope**.  
- **Electrons** are the fans cheering from the stands. If some fans leave or extra fans arrive, the crowd’s overall vibe (charge) changes, but the team (protons) stays the same. Adding or removing fans creates an **ion**.

## Worked example
Consider carbon:

1. **Neutral carbon atom**: 6 protons, 6 electrons, 6 neutrons.  
   $$\text{C}: \; p=6,\; e=6,\; n=6$$

2. **Isotope carbon‑14**: same 6 protons, same 6 electrons, but 8 neutrons.  
   $$\text{^{14}C}: \; p=6,\; e=6,\; n=8$$  
   Mass increases, but the charge remains zero.

3. **Ion C⁺** (a carbon cation): 6 protons, 5 electrons, 6 neutrons.  
   $$\text{C}^{+}: \; p=6,\; e=5,\; n=6$$  
   One electron removed, giving a +1 charge.

4. **Ion C⁻** (a carbon anion): 6 protons, 7 electrons, 6 neutrons.  
   $$\text{C}^{-}: \; p=6,\; e=7,\; n=6$$  
   One extra electron, giving a –1 charge.

Notice how only neutrons change for isotopes, while only electrons change for ions.

## Try this
A sodium atom has 11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 11 electrons. Write the notation for:

1. The isotope with 13 neutrons.  
2. The ion that has lost one electron.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. $^{24}\text{Na}$ (11 p + 13 n = mass number 24)  
2. $\text{Na}^{+}$ (11 p, 11 e → 10 e, charge +1)
