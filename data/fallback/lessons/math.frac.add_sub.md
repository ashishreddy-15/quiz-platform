## The idea  
When two fractions have the same denominator, you keep that denominator and add or subtract only the numerators.  
Example: \(\dfrac{3}{8}+\dfrac{2}{8}=\dfrac{3+2}{8}=\dfrac{5}{8}\).  
If the denominators differ, first make them equal (find a common denominator) before adding or subtracting.

## Why your answer happened  
You likely added the numerators and denominators together, e.g. \(\dfrac{3}{8}+\dfrac{2}{8}=\dfrac{5}{16}\).  
That mistake comes from thinking “add the top and the bottom” works for all fractions.  
It feels reasonable because it looks like a simple addition, but it ignores that the denominator represents the same size pieces.

## Analogy  
Think of a basketball team’s points.  
The *denominator* is the total number of baskets in a game.  
The *numerator* is how many of those baskets a player scored.  
If two players scored from the same number of shots (same denominator), you just add their scored baskets (numerators).  
If they had different numbers of shots, you first compare the same number of shots (make denominators equal) before adding baskets.

## Worked example  
Add \(\dfrac{1}{6}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{4}\).

1. Find a common denominator:  
   \(\operatorname{lcm}(6,4)=12\).

2. Convert each fraction:  
   \[
   \dfrac{1}{6}= \dfrac{1\times2}{6\times2}= \dfrac{2}{12},\qquad
   \dfrac{1}{4}= \dfrac{1\times3}{4\times3}= \dfrac{3}{12}.
   \]

3. Add the numerators:  
   \[
   \dfrac{2}{12}+\dfrac{3}{12}= \dfrac{2+3}{12}= \dfrac{5}{12}.
   \]

The answer is \(\boxed{\dfrac{5}{12}}\).

## Try this  
Add \(\dfrac{2}{5}\) and \(\dfrac{3}{10}\).

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
First common denominator \(10\).  
\(\dfrac{2}{5}= \dfrac{4}{10}\).  
\(\dfrac{4}{10}+\dfrac{3}{10}= \dfrac{7}{10}\).  
So the sum is \(\boxed{\dfrac{7}{10}}\).
