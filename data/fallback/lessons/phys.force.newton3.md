## The idea  
When two objects interact, each exerts a force on the other.  
The forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but they act on **different** objects.  
Mathematically: if object A exerts force **F** on object B, then B exerts force –**F** on A.

## Why your answer happened  
Many students think the forces act on the same object, so they write “the force on A equals the force on A.”  
This feels reasonable because we often feel a reaction (like a kick) on the same body that applies the push.  
In reality, the reaction acts on the other body, not on the one that applied the force.

## Analogy  
Think of a soccer ball (object B) and a player’s foot (object A).  
The foot pushes the ball forward; that push is like a hand‑shake.  
The ball pushes the foot backward with the same strength.  
The foot and ball are the two people shaking hands; the force on the foot is the reaction on the other person.

## Worked example  
A 70 kg player (A) kicks a 0.5 kg ball (B) with a speed of 15 m s⁻¹.  
Assume the kick lasts 0.05 s.

1. Momentum change of the ball:  
   \[
   \Delta p_B = m_B v_B = 0.5\,\text{kg}\times15\,\text{m s}^{-1}=7.5\,\text{kg m s}^{-1}
   \]
2. Force on the ball:  
   \[
   F_{AB} = \frac{\Delta p_B}{\Delta t}= \frac{7.5}{0.05}=150\,\text{N}
   \]
3. Reaction force on the player:  
   \[
   F_{BA} = -F_{AB} = -150\,\text{N}
   \]
   (negative sign indicates opposite direction).

Thus the ball feels +150 N forward, the player feels –150 N backward.

## Try this  
A 2 kg sled is pulled by a 5 kg person with a force of 30 N.  
What is the magnitude of the force the sled exerts on the person?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>30 N (equal and opposite)</details>
