## The idea  
Newton’s second law says that the net force acting on an object equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration:  
\[
\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}} = m\,\mathbf{a}.
\]  
Acceleration is the change of velocity over time, not the velocity itself. A larger force or a smaller mass produces a larger acceleration.

## Why your answer happened  
Many students think “force equals mass times velocity.” That feels right because both force and velocity are vectors, and multiplying by mass seems natural. However, velocity is how fast an object moves, while acceleration tells us how fast that speed changes. The law connects force to the change in motion, not the motion itself.

## Analogy  
Imagine a soccer ball in a game.  
- The **mass** of the ball is like its weight—how heavy it feels.  
- The **force** you apply with your foot is like the kick you give the ball.  
- The **acceleration** is how quickly the ball speeds up after the kick.  
So, a heavier ball (more mass) needs a stronger kick (greater force) to reach the same speed change as a lighter ball.

## Worked example  
A 0.5 kg ball is kicked so that its velocity changes from 0 m/s to 10 m/s in 0.2 s.  

1. Find acceleration:  
   \[
   a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{10-0}{0.2} = 50\ \text{m/s}^2.
   \]
2. Apply Newton’s second law:  
   \[
   F_{\text{net}} = m\,a = 0.5 \times 50 = 25\ \text{N}.
   \]
So a net force of 25 N is required to produce that change in speed.

## Try this  
What force is needed to accelerate a 2 kg rugby ball from rest to 5 m/s in 0.5 s?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
\[
a = \frac{5-0}{0.5}=10\ \text{m/s}^2,\qquad
F_{\text{net}} = 2\times10 = 20\ \text{N}.
\]
</details>
